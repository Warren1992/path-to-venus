Can a Balloon Survive the Night on Venus?

The Vega balloons floated in Venus’s clouds in 1985. A future robotic balloon would have to do something harder: stay in a useful layer through the dark, then get back to work at sunrise.

Why a balloon’s night is not 117 Earth days

Imagine two clocks on Venus. One is bolted to the ground and measures the time from one local sunrise to the next: roughly 117 Earth days. The other is riding a balloon, swept westward by an atmosphere that circles the planet far faster than the solid world turns. This fast movement of the air relative to the surface is called superrotation. The two clocks are not keeping the same schedule.

In a NASA-sponsored Venus Flagship Mission study, winds would carry an uncrewed balloon around Venus about once every five Earth days. If the balloon spent roughly half a circuit out of sunlight at a steady speed, darkness would last around two and a half Earth days. That is a rough illustration, not a forecast: wind speed, flight path and altitude can change the time between sunset and sunrise. A mission would need to plan for a longer night than its average estimate.

The practical answer: Venus balloons have already floated for almost two Earth days. Making it through repeated day–night cycles while holding a useful altitude and returning data is a different test. A balloon’s lift, power supply and ability to communicate must each survive the crossing.

What the Vega balloons actually proved

In 1985, the Soviet Vega 1 and Vega 2 missions each released a helium balloon into the Venus clouds. They drifted at about 54 kilometers (34 miles) above the surface. According to NASA’s aerial-platform study, each traveled approximately 11,500 kilometers during its roughly two days of tracked flight, close to the distance from New York to Tokyo.

They were superpressure balloons: their sealed envelopes maintained a nearly fixed volume across normal temperature and pressure changes. That let each stay near a target altitude without continually running a motor. They still moved up and down in the atmosphere, and they were not designed to travel deliberately to a new cloud layer. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory puts the end of their transmissions just beyond 46 hours, when the instrument batteries ran out. That tells us when the record ended, not when the balloons fell.

What changes after sunset?

The balloon may keep floating while the science shuts down

Buoyancy is not the same thing as electrical power. A passive balloon does not have to burn fuel or spin rotors simply to stay aloft. But instruments, radios and an active altitude-control system all need electricity. Once a solar-powered balloon enters darkness, it has to run from stored energy. The flagship study planned to charge the batteries before the first night, reduce or suspend some altitude changes after dark, and store measurements to transmit later. A solar balloon could spend the night collecting useful data without trying to do everything it does in daylight.

Altitude is a range, not a point

A balloon floats when the weight of the atmosphere it displaces matches its own total weight. Rise into thinner air and the same volume displaces less mass; descend into denser air and it displaces more. A flexible balloon can also change volume as its lifting gas warms or cools. A superpressure balloon limits that change as long as its envelope stays taut. Even so, moving air, a leak or loss of enough pressure to slacken the envelope could push the vehicle outside its intended range. Designers care less about holding an exact number on the altimeter than staying where the structure and instruments can still work.

JPL and its partners have tested a different approach on Earth: a helium balloon with a pressurized inner reservoir and a flexible outer envelope. Moving helium into the outer envelope increases the volume of air the balloon displaces and makes it rise; moving helium back into the inner reservoir lets it descend. The proposed Venus science range is about 52 to 62 kilometers (32 to 39 miles). Flights over Nevada demonstrated altitude control in some comparable conditions, not survival through a complete Venus night or repeated exposure to its sulfuric-acid clouds.

What would count as a success on Venus?

For a first full test, look for three things in the data after the next sunrise:

  1. A continuous altitude record showing that the balloon stayed within a usable range, including under the coldest conditions it encountered.
  2. A power record showing it kept critical electronics working in darkness, then recharged after sunrise. An orbiter could receive a stored log later; the balloon would not need to stay in constant radio contact.
  3. Another successful science measurement after sunrise. A brief radio ping is evidence of contact, but not proof that the scientific payload or its altitude controls survived.

One successful cycle would prove more than a flight of similar length that happened to end before sunrise. Repeating the test would show how performance changes as the equipment ages. It would not establish that people can live there, but it would tell us whether a robot can work through the rhythms of a moving Venus sky.
